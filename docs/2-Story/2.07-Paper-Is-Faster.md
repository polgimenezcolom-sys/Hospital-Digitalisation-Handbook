# *"The pharmacist says paper is faster"*

You have the pharmacy module working. You demonstrate it: search the patient, find the prescription,
select the drug, enter the quantity, confirm, save.

The pharmacist watches politely and says it is very good. Then she says that at the moment she writes
one line in a book and hands over the tablets, and there are forty people outside.

She is not being difficult. **She is right**, and if you argue with her you will lose — not the
argument, the deployment.

## Do the arithmetic she just did

A hospital information system out of the box is built for a large institution with specialised staff.
The pharmacy module in a full ERP has stock locations, procurement, quotations, transfer orders,
approval workflows and a dozen fields the vendor's other customers need.

Dispensing one drug through that interface might be twelve clicks across three screens. Writing a
line in a book is about eight seconds.

Multiply by a hundred and fifty patients a day and the choice is not close. No amount of explaining
the long-term benefits of integrated stock management changes an arithmetic problem.

**So change the arithmetic.**

## Strip it down until it fits

The single most effective technical intervention available to you is not adding features. It is
deleting them.

At Lunsar the pharmacy interface was cut down to a two-screen dispensing flow: find the patient, hand
over the drug. Everything else — procurement, quotations, transfers, invoicing, the entire
purchasing side of the ERP — was hidden. Not removed from the database; hidden from the person doing
the job.

The same principle applied elsewhere:

- The consultation screen was reduced to observations, diagnosis, disposition and summary. Nothing
  else.
- The stock system's menu was cut to three items — dashboard, inventory, products.
- Reports were trimmed to the ones that describe this hospital. Roughly forty-four stock demo
  reports were removed, because they queried concepts this hospital does not record and would have
  returned errors or empty tables. A report that returns nothing teaches a user that the system is
  broken.

!!! tip "The rule"

    **Every field on the screen has to earn its place by being used, today, by this person.**

    A field that is optional will be left blank. A field that is mandatory and pointless will be
    filled with rubbish. Both outcomes are worse than not having the field.

## Add complexity later, or never

The phased model exists for this reason. You are not building the final system. You are building the
smallest thing that is genuinely better than paper for one process, and then leaving.

Phase 2 is not "the same screen with more fields". It is a *later trip*, after staff have used
phase 1 for six months and have started asking for things. Requests that come from users are worth
ten times what you would have designed for them.

And sometimes the answer is that the complexity never arrives. If a stripped-down dispensing screen
is still what the pharmacy needs in three years, that is a success, not an unfinished project.

## Two ways to do integration, and one of them is a trap

At some point you will want the prescription written by the clinician to appear automatically in
pharmacy and decrement stock. There are two ways to get there.

**Tight integration.** The systems talk directly, synchronising automatically. Elegant when it works.
It requires both systems to be up, the network between them to be reliable, and someone to
understand the sync when it breaks — which it will, at 2 a.m., in a language nobody on site reads.

**Decoupled.** The clinician prescribes in the clinical system. The pharmacist reads a list of
pending prescriptions and records the dispense. Two systems, one deliberate human step between them,
around thirty seconds per transaction.

The decoupled version is *worse* on paper and *better* in a building where the power is unstable, the
network is a mesh, and there is no database administrator within five hundred kilometres. If one
system is down, the other keeps working. Nothing silently desynchronises. Nobody needs to debug a
message queue.

This is the central design argument of the whole project: **operational pragmatism over technical
elegance.** Choose the architecture that degrades gracefully, not the one that demos well.

!!! warning "From the archive"

    Both approaches were built and documented at Lunsar so they could be compared — a tightly
    integrated ERP path and a decoupled one where the pharmacist reads a report and records the
    movement manually. The comparison is in the thesis. The decoupled version is the one that
    survives a bad week.
