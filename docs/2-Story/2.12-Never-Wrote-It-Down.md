# *"We never wrote down what we did"*

It is the last afternoon. The flight is tomorrow, there is a goodbye lunch at two, and there are
four things still not finished. Somebody suggests writing the documentation on the plane.

Nobody writes documentation on the plane.

## What happens instead

Eleven months later, a new team lands. They have the same enthusiasm you had and none of your
context. They find a server whose password nobody knows, a network whose layout was in your head, a
configuration whose reasoning is gone, and a hospital that has been asked to be patient about the
same thing three times.

So they do the sensible thing. They start again.

That is not a hypothetical. It is the most reliably reproducible result in eight years of this
project, and it is the reason this handbook exists.

## One variable

We went back through the whole archive looking for what separated the deployments that were built on
from the ones that were rebuilt. It was not the software, the budget, or the quality of the
engineering.

It was whether the departing team left a written handover.

- The 2018 academic system: no pack. The closure report says it was installed and the administrator
  trained. Six years later the hospital's IT technician described it as a plan that was never
  implemented. Both records exist. Nobody wrote down which is true, and now nobody can.
- The 2023 form system: no pack. Two of eleven forms had any data a year later.
- The 2024 edition: **left a pack** — an index of what they inherited, with honest commentary on each
  earlier edition. It is the only document of its kind in eight years. The two editions that followed
  inherited it and built on it.

One document, written by one cohort who happened to care.

## What goes in it

Not a report of what you achieved. A pack for the person who has to live with it.

**The named owner.** Who is responsible now. Role, name, contact. If this line is blank, the rest
does not matter.

**A runbook per deployed unit.** How to do the daily task, with screenshots, written for someone who
has never used a computer. Test it: hand it to someone who was not in your training and watch them
try. Every place they hesitate is a missing step.

**"What to do when it breaks."** The five most likely failures and the first thing to try for each.
The server does not respond. The screen is blank. Nobody can log in. The report is empty. The printer
does nothing. This one page will be used more than everything else combined.

**Credentials, handed over securely.** In person, separately, recorded for rotation — and *not* in
the runbook, the shared folder, or anything that might become a thesis appendix.

**The support ladder.** Tier 0, 1 and 2 with real names and real routes.
([Chapter 11](2.11-Nobody-Used-It.md).)

**Backup, demonstrated.** Not documented — demonstrated. Watch the owner do a backup and a restore
while you are still in the building. A backup that has never been restored is a belief, not a backup.

**The cutover plan**, if paper is being retired after you leave: the date, who announces it, what
happens on the day. ([Chapter 8](2.08-Doing-Both.md).)

**And the page that matters most: what you deliberately did *not* do, and why.**

## Write down what you did not do

This is the section teams skip and the next team needs most.

Every deployment has things that were considered and rejected, half-built and abandoned, or simply
ran out of time. Written down, they are the next team's scope — a running start. Left unwritten, the
next team rediscovers each one at the cost of a week apiece, and reaches the same conclusions you
did.

Be specific and be honest:

- *"We did not deploy the billing module. The hospital runs an existing accounting system and
  reconciling with it is a bigger job than we had time for. Anyone taking this on should start by
  asking the administrator what that system exports."*
- *"We could not confirm whether the network reaches Emergency. The groundings are installed; we
  could not verify the cable. This blocks Emergency in any future phase and someone should check it
  physically."*
- *"We considered building a simplified interface over the pharmacy system and did not, because the
  authentication between the two was unresolved. The analysis is in the repository."*

That is a gift. It is also the entire difference between a project and a series of attempts.

!!! warning "Honesty over polish"

    A handover that only records successes is close to useless, and everyone can tell. The next team
    will hit the same problems you did — your write-up is the only thing that lets them skip the week
    you lost.

    AUCOOP's [Community Network Handbook](https://aucoop.github.io/Community-Network-Handbook/contributing/case-study/)
    puts it well, and its case-study template is the one to follow.

## Do it on Wednesday

The handover pack is not a last-day task. Start it in the middle of the second week, while the
reasoning is fresh and there is still time to fix what the writing exposes — because writing the
runbook is how you discover the step that only works when you do it.

Then walk the owner through it, in person, before the goodbye lunch.
