# *"The power went out mid-consultation"*

It is Tuesday afternoon. A clinician is halfway through entering an observation. The lights go, the
fan stops, and the screen dies with it.

The generator starts about ninety seconds later. Everything comes back. The consultation is
re-entered from memory, one more person decides this is not worth the trouble, and — invisibly — the
database may now have a half-written transaction in it.

That last part is the one nobody sees, and it is the one that will hurt you.

## Three different problems wearing one coat

"Unreliable power" is really three separate failures, and they need three separate answers. Solve one
and you have solved none.

**Outage.** The grid stops. This is the visible one. In many rural hospitals grid supply runs for
only part of the day, and a generator covers some of the rest, when there is fuel.

**Voltage instability.** Far more damaging and almost invisible. Grid voltage in these settings swings
roughly between **170 V and 260 V** against a nominal 220–240 V. Equipment does not switch off — it
runs badly, and then one day the power supply in your server fails and nobody connects the two
events.

**Unclean shutdown.** A machine losing power mid-write is not the same as a machine being switched
off. Database engines are reasonably good at recovering from this, and "reasonably good" is not the
standard you want for clinical records.

## What a hospital deployment actually needs

Three layers, in this order of importance:

1. **Voltage regulation.** A UPS with automatic voltage regulation (AVR) that corrects the sag rather
   than passing it through. This protects the hardware, and it is the layer people most often skip
   because the problem is invisible until it isn't.
2. **Battery backup with enough runtime to shut down cleanly** — or to bridge to the generator. You
   are not trying to keep working through a four-hour outage. You are trying to make sure the machine
   is never surprised.
3. **Independent generation**, where there is no reliable generator — usually solar, sized for the IT
   load only, not for the building.

The sizing arithmetic is simple and it is in [the guide](../3-Guide/Power.md). Total up what you are
actually plugging in, apply the UPS formula, and add headroom. A typical small hospital IT load —
one server, a PoE switch with its budget, four access points, a handful of laptops and a backup
drive — comes to something under 800 W, which is a smaller UPS than most people expect.

## The part that is not about electricity

Here is the thing that catches teams out: **the server is not the fragile part. The workstation is.**

Everyone protects the server. It is in a room, it feels important, it gets the UPS. Meanwhile the
registration desk runs on a desktop plugged straight into the wall, and that is the machine a clerk
is typing into when the power goes.

Prefer laptops over desktops at the point of care, for exactly one reason: a laptop has a battery, so
a power cut is an inconvenience rather than a data-loss event. It is a boring recommendation and it
prevents more grief than any amount of server engineering.

## What to record while you are there

Power is one of the things you genuinely cannot assess from home, so measure it properly:

- **Grid hours per day**, observed across the days you are there — not what you were told.
- **Voltage over 24 hours**, with a multimeter. The spread matters more than the average.
- **Generator**: fuel type, capacity, actual runtime, and — this is the real question — how reliably
  fuel is available.
- **Per building**: where the sockets are, which circuits they are on, what else is on that circuit.
- If there is solar: panel count, orientation, rating, battery capacity and age.
