# *"Nobody can find what happened last time she came"*

It is your second morning. You are standing at the back of the outpatient department, which today
means a corridor with a bench along one wall and around a hundred and thirty people who will be seen
before dark.

A woman reaches the front. She was here in March. She remembers being given tablets, and she
remembers being told to come back. She does not remember the name of the drug, and she does not have
the small card she was given, because that was five months and one rainy season ago.

The clerk asks her name. There are four spellings of it in the register. There are three other women
with the same name. Somewhere in the hospital there is a folder with her history in it, and finding
it means knowing a number that nobody can now reconstruct.

The clinician sees her, examines her, and starts again.

## What just happened

That is the problem you were sent here to solve, and it is worth being precise about what it actually
is, because it is not "the hospital has no computers."

The hospital has an excellent memory. Every consultation is written down, carefully, in a bound
register. Every admission is recorded. The monthly returns are accurate and they are submitted on
time. **The information is not missing — it is unreachable at the one moment it has any value.**

Digitalising a hospital is not about creating records. The records exist. It is about making a record
findable by the person holding the patient's arm.

## The folder ID is the whole system

At Lunsar, when a patient registers for the first time, Reception mints a number and writes it on a
card. That number goes on the patient's folder. It goes at the top of the ward form, the laboratory
request, the theatre list, the dressing register.

That number is the join key of the entire hospital.

Every paper system in the building is held together by it. Which means three things for you:

1. **It is not a legacy detail to be migrated away from.** It is the identifier the hospital thinks
   in. Staff will keep using it whether or not your software does.
2. **In whatever you deploy, it must be the primary searchable identifier** — the thing you type into
   the search box — not a secondary attribute buried in a demographics tab. If a clerk has to search
   by name because the folder ID is three clicks deep, they will search by name, and you will get
   duplicate patients within a week.
3. **Registration is a single event, not a per-unit one.** Identity is created once, at Reception.
   Every other unit consumes it. Getting this backwards — letting each department register its own
   patients — produces four records for one woman by lunchtime.

!!! warning "From the archive"

    Many patients arrive with no national identity document of any kind. The hospital-assigned number
    written on a card is, for a large proportion of them, the only durable link between a person and
    their medical history — and it is a piece of paper in a pocket, in a climate with a rainy season.

    This is why Phase 1 is registration, and why it is not the boring phase.

## Why this is the phase to start with

There is a real temptation to start somewhere more visibly clinical — a nice consultation form, a
laboratory module, something a doctor will thank you for. Resist it.

Registration is the right first phase for three reasons that all point the same way:

- **It is where identity is created.** Every later phase depends on being able to attach data to the
  right person. Build laboratory before registration and you have results that belong to nobody in
  particular.
- **It is one desk, one workflow, two or three staff.** You can train it properly in the time you
  have, watch it being used, and fix what is wrong before you leave.
- **It is the one process where the digital path can plausibly become the only path** within a single
  trip — which, as [chapter 8](2.08-Doing-Both.md) explains, is the only kind of migration that
  actually sticks.

## What this means for your week

Before you configure anything, sit at Reception for two hours and watch. Specifically:

- Watch a **new** patient being registered. How long does it take? What is written, and where?
- Watch a **returning** patient. How is the folder found? What happens when it is not found?
- Ask the clerk what goes wrong most often. Then ask what they do when it goes wrong.
- Find out whether folder numbers are ever reused, and whether different units keep their own
  separate numbering. (If they do, you have discovered something important and you should write it
  down immediately.)
