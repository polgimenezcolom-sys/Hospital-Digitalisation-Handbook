# *"We agreed it with the director. Nobody told the nurses."*

The meeting went beautifully. The medical director was welcoming, said the hospital had wanted this
for years, and told you to go ahead with whatever you needed.

On Thursday you arrive at the general ward with two tablets and a plan. The nurse in charge has not
heard of the project. She is mid-drug-round, three staff short, and a stranger has appeared wanting
her to change how she records admissions. She is polite. She is also not going to do it.

She is right, and the failure is yours.

## Permission is not support

The director gave you **permission**. That is real and you needed it, but it is not the same as an
instruction to the ward, a slot in the week when staff are released to learn, or any consequence for
the system not being used.

A hospital is not a company. The director's authority over how a senior nurse actually records her
admissions is far weaker than an organisational chart suggests, and it should be — clinical
judgement is hers. Nothing you install changes that.

So what you need is not one yes. It is a chain of them.

## The six people

Find all of these in your first week, by name, and speak to each one separately. A group meeting
produces agreement and no commitment.

| Who | Why they decide whether this works |
|---|---|
| **Medical Director** | The only person who can make this an expectation rather than a suggestion — and who must say so to staff himself, not through you. |
| **Head of Nursing** | Nurses do the largest volume of data entry in any hospital. If nursing is not with you, nothing on the wards will happen. |
| **Head of Pharmacy** | Owns stock. Will be the first to notice if dispensing gets slower, and the first to stop. |
| **Laboratory Director** | Owns the result chain — the place where digitalisation usually delivers its most visible early win. |
| **Administrator / Finance** | Owns billing and the monthly returns. Ask what finance system already exists; there is almost certainly one, and it is older than you expect. |
| **IT Officer** | Becomes your super-user, your counterpart, and the person this all belongs to when you leave. If this post does not exist, go back to [chapter 3](2.03-Should-We-Deploy.md). |

Ask each of them the same two questions:

1. **What takes too long in your unit right now?**
2. **What would have to be true for this to be worth your staff's time?**

The answers will not match what the director told you, and the gap between them is the most useful
information you will collect all trip.

## Put it in writing

Before configuration starts, there should be a short written agreement — a memorandum of
understanding — between the deploying organisation and the hospital. It does not need to be legal. It
needs to be specific, and it needs to name people.

It should cover:

- **Who owns the system.** The hospital. Say it explicitly.
- **Who owns the data**, and how patient consent is handled.
- **Training commitments** — how many staff, released for how long, and when.
- **Remote support** — what the deploying organisation will and will not do after leaving, and for
  how long.
- **Who pays for what, after you go.** Hosting, hardware replacement, the IT post.

That last line is the one people skip, and it is the one that predicts survival.

!!! warning "From the archive"

    At St. Jean de Dieu in Yassa–Douala, the hospital's administrative board had already prioritised
    digitalisation before the team arrived. Rooms were made available for training, staff time was
    released, and the project had visible institutional backing. There was also a pair of local
    champions — the hospital's IT technician and the director himself.

    Douala is the site that adopted.

    It is a one-year comparison, not a controlled experiment, and the board was not the only
    difference. But it points the same way as everything else in the literature, and the same way as
    common sense.

## Find your super-users on day two, not day twelve

Somewhere in the hospital are one or two people who are curious about this, respected by their
colleagues, and permanent staff. Not the most senior. Not the most technical. The ones people already
ask when something on a phone stops working.

Find them early and build the whole deployment around them. They should be in the room for
configuration decisions, not just for training at the end. When you leave, knowledge has to live in a
person on a ward — not only in a document, and certainly not only in you.

!!! danger "Stop"

    If, by the end of week one, you cannot name the person who will still be responsible for this in
    a year, that is the finding of your trip. Report it as such. It matters more than anything you
    could have installed.
