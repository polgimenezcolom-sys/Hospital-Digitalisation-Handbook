# *"They're doing both now"*

Two weeks in. Registration is going well — you can see the records appearing. You are pleased.

Then you notice the clerk, after saving the record on screen, reach for the bound register and write
the same entry again in pen.

You ask why. She says: *in case.*

## This is the failure mode

Not rejection. Not technical collapse. **Duplication.**

Every one of the digital systems this project has deployed since 2018 ended the same way, and none of
them was ever switched off. Paper simply was never switched off either. And once two systems run in
parallel, the ending is arithmetic:

1. Staff do the work twice. Nobody has time to do the work twice.
2. So one of them gets skipped when the department is busy — and the one that gets skipped is
   whichever is slower, less familiar, or needs electricity.
3. The digital record becomes incomplete.
4. Because it is incomplete, nobody trusts it, so nobody uses it for anything that matters.
5. Because nobody uses it for anything that matters, there is no reason to keep filling it in.

Six months later it is dark, and no decision was ever taken.

The clerk's *in case* is completely rational. She has been asked to rely on something that has been in
the building for three weeks, that stopped working on Tuesday when the power went, and that the
people who installed it are about to fly away from. Of course she keeps the book.

**The problem is not her caution. It is that you asked her to make a judgement call you should have
removed.**

## The design rule

> **The unit of migration is the process, not the institution. A process moves to digital only when
> the digital path can become the *only* path.**

Read that again, because it is the most important sentence in this handbook.

You are not digitising "the hospital". You are moving *one process* — registration, say — and you move
it only when you can say, on a specific date, that the paper register for that process is closed.
Not discouraged. Closed. Put in a cupboard.

If you cannot say that, you are not ready to move that process, and you should be working on the
reason you cannot.

## What has to be true before you can close the register

Every one of these, for the specific process you are retiring:

- **It works when the power is out.** Either the device has a battery, or the process can wait, or
  there is an agreed paper fallback *with a defined route back into the system* — which is different
  from a parallel register.
- **Everyone on every shift can do it.** Including the person who covers nights, and the person who
  is on leave this week and comes back to a changed process.
- **It is not slower than what it replaces.** See [chapter 7](2.07-Paper-Is-Faster.md).
- **Somebody at the hospital can fix the common failures** without calling Barcelona.
- **The director has set the date and announced it.** Not you. The hospital retires its own register.

Then you pick a date, announce it a week ahead, and on that date the book goes away.

!!! danger "Stop"

    If you are leaving in five days and you have not retired a single paper process, do not retire
    one now in a hurry.

    A cutover with nobody around to support it for the first fortnight is worse than an honest
    handover that says: *phase 1 is configured and trained, the cutover date is set for six weeks
    after our departure, and here is exactly what has to happen on that day.*

    Write the cutover plan into the [handover pack](../4-Instruments/L1-Handover-Pack.md). Give the
    named owner the authority and the checklist to run it.

## Start with the one process you can actually close

This is the real reason [registration is the right first phase](2.01-Last-Time-She-Came.md). It is
one desk, a small number of staff, a single well-defined event, and the digital path can genuinely
become the only path within one deployment.

Compare that with trying to digitise "the wards": twenty staff across three shifts, several
buildings, half a dozen forms, and no realistic date on which paper stops. You will get parallel
systems by default, because there is no moment at which the change completes.

**Narrow enough to finish beats broad enough to impress.**
