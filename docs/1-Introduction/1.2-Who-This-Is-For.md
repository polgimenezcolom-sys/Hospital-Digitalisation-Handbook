# Who this is for

This handbook is for people who are about to do the work, not for people studying it.

You do **not** need a health background, a clinical qualification, or any prior experience with
hospital software. You do need to be willing to spend the first week of a three-week trip watching
people work instead of installing things.

We assume:

- **Basic technical confidence** — you can open a terminal, follow a command, and read a config file
  without panicking.
- **That you will ask questions** — of nurses, clerks, pharmacists, the person who fills in the
  monthly return. Most of what matters is not written anywhere.
- **A real counterpart** — a hospital that has asked for this, not one you have decided needs it.

## Three readers

Over the years the same three people keep turning up. You will probably recognise yourself.

### The volunteer engineer

A student or a recent graduate, dropping into a project for two or three weeks. You are competent,
you are motivated, and you have never worked in a hospital. Your instinct will be to start
installing on day one.

**Start here:** read the whole [story](../2-Story/index.md) before you fly — it is about an hour, and
it is the difference between deploying something that survives and deploying something that gets
quietly abandoned. Then use the [guide](../3-Guide/index.md) on site.

**Your specific risk:** building something impressive that nobody asked for. The most common failure
in this project's history is not incompetence — it is a well-engineered system solving a problem the
hospital did not have.

### The project coordinator

You are organising the deployment: budget, partners, the relationship with the hospital, the report
that has to be written afterwards. You may not be the person typing commands.

**Start here:** [*"Should we be deploying this at all?"*](../2-Story/2.03-Should-We-Deploy.md), then
[*"Nobody told the nurses"*](../2-Story/2.04-Nobody-Told-The-Nurses.md), then the
[instruments](../4-Instruments/index.md) — B-1, B-2 and B-3 are yours, and they should be finished
before anyone books a flight.

**Your specific risk:** agreeing scope with one enthusiastic person at the hospital and discovering
in week two that the wards were never consulted.

### The hospital's IT person

You work here. The volunteers will leave; you will not. Everything in this handbook eventually
becomes your responsibility, and that is the point.

**Start here:** the [story](../2-Story/index.md) chapters that match your situation, then the
[handover pack](../4-Instruments/L1-Handover-Pack.md) — that is the document the team owes you, and
you are entitled to insist on it before they go.

**Your specific risk:** being handed a system nobody explained, with credentials nobody wrote down,
and being expected to keep it alive. If that is happening, say so while the team is still in the
country.

## What you will be able to do

By the time you have worked through this handbook you should be able to:

- Judge whether a hospital is ready for a hospital information system at all — and say no when it is
  not, which is a legitimate and sometimes correct outcome.
- Map how a patient actually moves through the hospital, and where the paper record is created,
  copied and lost.
- Record enough about the site, in the days you are there, that questions arising six months later
  can still be answered.
- Deploy and configure Bahmni or GNU Health for a phase of hospital operations, and know which phase
  to start with.
- Train the different people who will use it — a nurse, a pharmacist, a clerk and a director need
  different things.
- Leave behind a system that a named person at the hospital can operate, and a written record that
  the next team can build on rather than repeat.
