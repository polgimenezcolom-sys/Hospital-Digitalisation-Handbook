# Why this handbook exists

A woman arrives at the outpatient department. She was seen here four months ago and was started on
treatment. The clinician needs to know what she was given, whether it worked, and what her last
laboratory results were.

The information exists. It is in a paper folder, somewhere, filed under a number written on a card
she may or may not still have. Finding it will take twenty minutes that the queue outside does not
allow. So the clinician does what any reasonable person does under that pressure: starts again.

That is the problem. Not that the hospital lacks computers — that a person's medical history is
functionally unreachable at the moment it is needed.

## This is not a new discovery

In July 2018, a UPC team met the administrator of St. John of God Hospital in Lunsar and wrote down
what he asked for. Three things: stock control, finance that worked with the accounting software they
already had, and — in his words, recorded in the report —

> clinical history, to make it easier to search for the information of recurrent patients, because
> now it is impossible for them to get the information of every patient that has come before.

The same 2018 report tabulated the candidate platforms: Odoo, GNU Health, OpenMRS. Advantages,
disadvantages, demo links.

Eight years later, a different UPC team ran the same comparison and arrived at essentially the same
shortlist. In the meantime, six volunteer editions came and went. Networks were installed. Forms were
digitised. Training was delivered and certified by a third party. And in July 2026, every statutory
monthly return the hospital submits — morbidity, bed state, mortality, immunisation, theatre,
laboratory — was still being filled in by hand.

**The requirements were understood in 2018. The technology was identified in 2018. What was missing
was never technical.**

## The three walls

AUCOOP's [Community Network Handbook](https://aucoop.github.io/Community-Network-Handbook/), written
by the networking side of the same association, describes exactly the walls we keep hitting, and
there is no point restating them differently:

- **The knowledge was scattered.** What one deployment learned lived in somebody's notes, a
  half-finished document, or in the memory of whoever happened to be there.
- **Every newcomer started from scratch.** In a student association people rotate out every year,
  taking the unwritten parts with them.
- **There was no shared toolset.** Each project picked its own tools, so volunteers from one
  deployment could not help another.

For a hospital information system there is a fourth, and it is the one that decides everything:

- **Nobody was responsible for it after we left.** Not because anyone was careless — because no
  person at the hospital was ever funded, named, and made accountable for keeping the system alive
  once the volunteers went home.

## What the archive actually shows

We went back through eight years of project files looking for the variable that separated the
deployments that survived from the ones that did not. It was not the software. It was not the budget,
and it was not the quality of the engineering, which was consistently good.

It was **whether the departing team left a written handover**.

| | Handover pack left? | What happened next |
|---|---|---|
| Academic management system, 2018 | No | The closure report records it installed and the administrator trained. Six years later the hospital's IT technician described it as a plan that was never implemented. Nobody wrote down which is true. |
| Form-collection system, 2023 | No | A review in January 2024 found 2 of 11 forms carrying any data at all — 568 submissions in total, every one of them made within two months of the training. |
| 2024 edition | **Yes** — an index of what they inherited, with commentary | Inherited and extended by the two editions that followed it. |

One document, written by one cohort who happened to care, is the difference between a project that
compounds and a project that restarts.

!!! warning "The failure mode is not rejection"

    Nobody at Lunsar ever refused to use a digital system. Nobody switched one off. Paper simply was
    never switched off either — so when the digital path became slower, or the power went, or the
    person who knew how to use it moved wards, everyone quietly fell back to the register that had
    always worked. The systems did not die. They went dark, and nobody noticed for a year.

## What this handbook is trying to do

Give a volunteer team, arriving for three weeks with good intentions and no institutional memory, the
accumulated result of eight years — so that the project starts from what is already known instead of
from zero, and so that what *this* team learns is captured well enough to be worth something to the
next one.

It will not make the work easy. It should make it possible to do the work once.
