# Choosing and setting up super-users

**Produces:** one or two named people who can answer nine questions out of ten after you leave, with
time allowance and accountability agreed in writing.
**When:** identified by day two, in the room for every configuration decision from then on.
**Instrument:** [B-3](../4-Instruments/B3-Mandate.md), section 2; [L-1](../4-Instruments/L1-Handover-Pack.md).

## Why this is not training

A super-user is not "the person who got the most training". It is a **role**: the first place a nurse
goes when something does not work, before the volunteer in Spain, before the IT technician. If that
role does not exist, every problem escalates to you, and the system lasts exactly as long as your
interest does.

## Who

Not the most senior. Not the most technical. The person people *already* ask when a phone does not
work. Criteria, in order:

1. **Permanent staff.** Not a volunteer, not a student, not someone on a contract that ends.
2. **Respected by colleagues** in their own unit. A super-user nobody asks is decorative.
3. **Curious.** They wanted to know how the thing worked before you offered to show them.
4. **Present across shifts**, or paired with someone who covers the other shift.

One per deployed unit is ideal; two for the hospital is the minimum. At Yassa–Douala the pair was the
IT technician and the director — a technical anchor and an authority anchor. That is a good shape.

## Steps

1. **Ask the heads of department** who they would send. Then watch for a day. The name you get and
   the person staff actually turn to are often different; you want the second.
2. **Ask the candidate.** Do they want this? It is extra work with no extra pay unless you arrange it.
   A reluctant super-user is worse than none.
3. **Agree the time allowance, in writing, with their head of department.** This is the step everyone
   skips. A super-user who is also a full-time nurse with no allowance will help until the ward gets
   busy, and then they will stop, and nobody will blame them. Two hours a week, protected, is enough
   to start.
4. **Put them in the room.** Every configuration decision from now on happens with them present.
   Which fields to remove, how the form is laid out, what the report looks like — they decide with
   you. Knowledge that lives only in a document is not knowledge; knowledge that lives in a person
   who made the decisions is.
5. **Train them beyond ordinary use.** Restarting the system. Adding a user. Resetting a password.
   Running a backup and — this matters — *restoring* one while you watch. What the five most likely
   failures look like and what to try first.
6. **Name them in the [handover pack](../4-Instruments/L1-Handover-Pack.md)** as tier 1 of the
   support ladder, with a contact route.
7. **Give them something to be accountable for.** *"The monthly return is produced from the system"*
   is a job. *"Look after the system"* is not.

## What goes wrong

**Choosing the IT technician by default.** Sometimes right, often not. The technician may be the
person who keeps the server alive, but the person a nurse asks about a form is a nurse. You need
both, and they are different jobs.

**No time allowance.** Named, trained, enthusiastic, and given no hours. See step 3. This is the
single most common way the role fails.

**Training the super-user last.** If they only appear at the training session at the end of the trip,
they are a well-trained user, not a super-user. Day two.

**Two super-users who are both on the day shift.** Nights and weekends are when things break and
nobody is around. Cover the roster, not the org chart.

!!! warning "From the archive"

    In early 2024 maintenance training was delivered at Lunsar to technical *and* management staff,
    documented, with a user manual, and certified by a partner institution. Adoption did not follow.
    Training was necessary and demonstrably insufficient. What was missing was not another session —
    it was a person whose job it was.
