# Stakeholder conversations

**Produces:** six interviews, each with the same two questions answered, and a list of what actually
takes too long — which will not match what the director told you.
**When:** first week on site, before configuring anything.
**Instrument:** [B-3](../4-Instruments/B3-Mandate.md), section 1.

## Why separately

A group meeting produces agreement and no commitment. Everyone nods, the most senior person speaks,
and you leave believing you have consensus. You have a room that did not want to disagree in front
of the director.

Speak to each of the six people **alone**, in their own unit, for twenty minutes. You will hear six
different problems, and the gaps between them are the most useful thing you learn all trip.

## The six, and why each one

| Who | Why they decide whether this works |
|---|---|
| **Medical Director** | The only person who can make it an expectation rather than a suggestion |
| **Head of Nursing** | Nurses do the largest volume of data entry in any hospital. If nursing is not with you, nothing on the wards happens |
| **Head of Pharmacy** | Owns stock. Will be the first to notice dispensing getting slower, and the first to stop |
| **Laboratory Director** | Owns the result chain — where digitalisation delivers its most visible early win |
| **Administrator / Finance** | Owns billing and the monthly returns. Knows what finance system already exists |
| **IT Officer / technician** | Becomes your super-user and, eventually, the owner. If the post does not exist, that is a stop-rule condition |

## The two questions

Ask exactly these, to everyone, and write the answers down in their words:

1. **What takes too long in your unit right now?**
2. **What would have to be true for this to be worth your staff's time?**

The first tells you what to build. The second tells you what will make them stop using it.

Do not pitch. Do not demonstrate. Do not explain what the system can do. Listen, and write. You will
get plenty of chances to show things later; you will not get a second chance at their honest answer
before they have seen you become invested in a solution.

## What to listen for

- **The person who compiles the monthly return.** Usually the matron, a records officer, or the
  administrator. They spend two or three days a month reading back through registers and counting by
  hand. They are your most useful ally and their deadline is a real recurring event you can attach
  the system to. See [*"The return is due on the 5th"*](../2-Story/2.09-Due-On-The-Fifth.md).
- **Anyone who says "we tried that before".** Ask what happened. Ask who ran it. Ask where it is now.
  At Lunsar this question would have surfaced the 2023 form system, the 2018 academic server, and
  the fact that nobody knew whether either was still in use.
- **The word "faster".** If a head of department says the current way is fast, believe them, and
  read [*"The pharmacist says paper is faster"*](../2-Story/2.07-Paper-Is-Faster.md) before you
  reply.
- **Who is *not* on the list.** The night-shift nurse in charge. The clerk who actually does
  registration, as opposed to the one who supervises it. Ask each person who else you should talk to.

## Steps

1. Book the six conversations in your first three days. Twenty minutes each, in their unit.
2. Ask the two questions. Write verbatim. Ask "who else should I talk to?" at the end.
3. On day four, lay the six answers side by side. Where they contradict the director's version, that
   is your real requirements list.
4. Feed the answers into [workflow mapping (D-3)](../4-Instruments/D3-Workflow-Mapping.md) — each
   named pain point should appear against a step in the patient journey.
5. Go back to the two or three people whose problem you are actually going to solve first, and tell
   them so. Specifically. *"We are starting with registration because you told us the folder search
   takes twenty minutes."* Being heard is the beginning of adoption.

## What goes wrong

**Talking to whoever is available.** The person with time to talk to you is often the person with the
least to do, and therefore the least representative. Insist on the six.

**Hearing what you came to hear.** You arrived with a system in mind. Every answer will sound like a
reason for it. Write the answers down *before* interpreting them, and have someone who was not in the
room read them.

**Skipping the IT technician because they are junior.** They are the person this all belongs to when
you leave. If they were not in the room for the decisions, they will not own the results.
