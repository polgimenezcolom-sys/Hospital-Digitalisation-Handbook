# The Handbook as a tool — does it do the job it exists to do?

*Analysis, v1.0 · 2026-09-09 · written against handbook v0.3*

**What this is.** Not a review of the writing. A functional analysis of the handbook — and above all
of its nine instruments — against the three tests that decide whether it is a working tool or a
well-argued document:

| | The test |
|---|---|
| **T1 · Field value** | Does completing this make the *hospital* better off faster, or prevent a costly wrong turn on this trip? |
| **T2 · Inheritance value** | Does the completed artefact give the *next cohort* what it needs, so they start from evidence rather than zero? |
| **T3 · Evaluative value** | Could someone in 2031, reading a stack of these across five cohorts, identify **which steps were key to survival and which omissions preceded obsolescence**? |

T3 is the one that matters most, because T3 is the thesis's own claim. A handbook that scores well on
T1 and T2 and badly on T3 is a good field guide that cannot demonstrate it is a good field guide —
which is precisely the epistemic position Bonet 2017 was in.

---

## 0. Verdict in one page

**T1 — Strong.** The set is well aimed. B-2's stop rule alone would have changed the Lunsar 2026
decision, and B-3's two questions and D-3's copy chain point the deployment at what the hospital
actually needs. Nothing here is busywork.

**T2 — Strong, and it is the part the handbook has thought hardest about.** D-1's provenance
discipline and L-1's "what we deliberately did not do" are the two best fields in the set. A 2027
cohort inheriting completed instruments would be materially better off than the 2026 cohort was.

**T3 — This is where the handbook currently fails, and it fails structurally rather than in detail.**

Five things are true at once, and together they mean that a 2031 analyst would be doing exactly what
the 2026 author had to do: reading a decade of prose end to end and reconstructing the pattern by
hand.

1. **The loop is drawn as a cycle and built as a line that stops two weeks after landing.** Every one
   of the nine instruments is completed between "before you fly" and "within two weeks of return".
   The variable the entire thesis rests on — survival — is measured in *years*. Three separate places
   (D-4 outcome 8, A-1 §5, L-1 §4) say "fill in at 6 and 12 months", and **no instrument, no owner,
   no trigger and no destination exists for that**. The team has dispersed; the students have
   graduated. As built, the handbook cannot produce a single data point about the thing it exists to
   fix.
2. **Nothing records what the team *inherited*.** Nine instruments capture what a team found at the
   hospital and what it did. None captures whether a previous handover pack existed, whether previous
   instruments were found, whether they were usable, what was missing. The handbook's central claim —
   handover correlates with survival — therefore stays a historical finding about 2016–2026 and can
   never be measured on new data.
3. **The stop rule has no override record**, so it cannot detect the failure it was built for. Lunsar
   2026 is the case: every stop-rule condition true, level 4 deployed anyway. Had B-2 existed, it
   would have been ticked and overridden — invisibly.
4. **The measurement layer is unoperationalised.** B-1's 0–10 scores have no anchors, so cohort A's 6
   is not cohort B's 6. D-4's eight outcomes have no definitions, denominators or methods, so two
   teams reporting "adoption 60%" are not reporting the same quantity.
5. **Everything except D-1's KoBo form is prose in a DOCX.** Nine free-text documents per trip, times
   five cohorts, is a folder someone has to read — which is the archive problem, reproduced by the
   instrument designed to fix it.

**The single most useful sentence in this analysis:** the handbook currently equips a team to *do* a
deployment well and to *hand over* well, but not to *be studied*. Four changes — listed in §5 — close
that, and none of them is large.

## 1. Instrument by instrument

Scores are `strong / adequate / weak / absent`, judged against what that instrument is *for*, not
against an ideal.

| | T1 field | T2 inherit | T3 evaluate | The binding constraint |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **B-1** Readiness | adequate | strong | **weak** | Scored once, before travel, and never again. Unanchored scale |
| **B-2** Level decision | **strong** | strong | adequate | No record of a stop-rule override |
| **B-3** Mandate | strong | **strong** | adequate | MoU captured as ticks, not as answers |
| **D-1** Data capture | adequate | **strong** | strong | Provenance column exists in one section of five |
| **D-2** Infrastructure | adequate | strong | weak | Entirely free text; trivially structurable |
| **D-3** Workflow | **strong** | strong | weak | Its one prediction is never checked back |
| **D-4** Exit gates | adequate | strong | **weak** | Eight outcomes, no operational definitions |
| **L-1** Handover | **strong** | **strong** | adequate | Cover sheet only; the pack itself has no templates |
| **A-1** Debrief | n/a | weak | **weak** | Asks about the handbook, not about the deployment or what it inherited |

### B-1 · Readiness assessment

The most analytically valuable instrument in the set, and the most crippled.

The open question everyone has focused on (E1, the thresholds) is **not the binding problem**. Two
others are worse:

- **It is scored once and never re-scored.** The design–reality gap is a *distance* that a good
  deployment should reduce. Scored once before travel it is a screening number. Scored again at
  departure and at twelve months it becomes an **outcome measure** — and the handbook suddenly has a
  quantitative dependent variable, comparable across cohorts and sites, that everything else can be
  correlated against. This is the cheapest available fix to the whole T3 problem: the form already
  exists, it just needs three columns instead of one.
- **The 0–10 scale has no anchors.** Nothing tells a scorer what a 3 looks like versus a 7 on
  "Management systems". Between two cohorts, and even between two people in one cohort, these numbers
  are not comparable — so any cross-cohort analysis built on them is noise wearing a number's
  clothing. One sentence per dimension per anchor point (0, 5, 10) fixes it, and it can be derived
  from Lunsar and Douala where you know the answers, which is the same exercise E1 needs anyway.

The four survival questions (`PAID-IT`, `OWNER-5Y`, `PRESENCE`, `HANDOVER`) are the best-designed
part of the instrument: binary, unambiguous, comparable, and each one traceable to an archive failure.
They are also the four fields most worth carrying into the structured record in §5.4.

### B-2 · Level decision

The highest field value of the nine. It is the instrument that would have changed 2026, and the case
study says so honestly.

It also contains **the only genuine prediction anywhere in the set**: *"If we deploy below what was
hoped: what has to become true for next year's answer to be higher?"* That is a falsifiable statement
with a named horizon — and nothing ever checks it. Neither A-1 nor next year's B-2 asks *"did last
year's condition come true?"* Closing that is one line in each form and it converts B-2 from a
decision record into a small longitudinal experiment.

**The gap that matters:** the stop rule is five tickboxes, any one of which means "drop a level or do
not deploy". There is no field for what happened when a box was ticked. The archive's central failure
is an *unrecorded override* — a level-4 system deployed at a level-1/2 site by careful engineers with
good reasons that nobody wrote down. An instrument that cannot record its own override cannot detect
the failure it exists to prevent. Add:

> **Stop-rule conditions ticked:** ☐☐☐☐☐ · **Deployed anyway?** Y / N · **Decided by:** ______ ·
> **On what grounds:** ______ · **What was put in place to compensate:** ______

Those five fields are, in my judgement, the single most analytically valuable addition available to
the whole instrument set.

### B-3 · Mandate and ownership

Well built, and the six-conversations design is right — the Douala/Lunsar contrast is real evidence
that institutional backing precedes adoption.

Two gaps, both of them recipe-to-instrument mismatches:

- The `Mandate` recipe's whole argument is that **permission ≠ instruction**, and that the test is
  whether the director will announce it to staff himself. The form never asks. Add: *"Did the
  director announce it to staff? To whom, when, in what form?"* That is the single field that
  distinguishes the two sites.
- The MoU section captures **whether a clause exists**, not **what it says**. "Who pays for what after
  we leave" is a tickbox. But the *answer* — hosting paid by whom, IT post funded or not, internet
  bill owned by whom — is what predicts survival. Capture the answers; the tick is worthless to a
  later reader.

### D-1 · Field data capture

The best-justified instrument in the handbook, and the KoBo XLSForm makes it the only structured,
queryable, photograph-attaching artefact in the set. The `counted / observed / asked` provenance field
is exactly right and should be the model for everything else.

**But the provenance discipline exists in only one of five sections.** The page's own headline is
*"record the source, not just the value"* — and the forms table, the identity section and the
power/network section have no source column. The bed count is the cautionary tale, and the bed count
would not have been caught by the current form, because the failure was not a missing number: it was
**an establishment figure and a census figure both being called "beds"**. Add a provenance column
throughout, and for any capacity figure force the distinction explicitly (*establishment / census /
physically counted*).

**One thing D-1 does not capture at all: the digital estate as found.** D-2 gives "existing software
systems from a previous project" a single line. That single line is the answer to the most common
inheritance question there is — what is already installed, what version, what is running, what died,
who has the credentials. At Lunsar in 2026 that question covered a 2018 academic server whose fate is
still disputed, a 2023 KoBo deployment, a 2024 Linux server, a web app and a mesh. One line.

### D-2 · Infrastructure audit

Sound and complete. The carrier-grade NAT check earning its own tip is a good example of the handbook
knowing which five minutes matter.

Its weakness is purely T3: it is entirely free text, and most of it is inherently numeric — grid
hours/day, measured bandwidth, voltage min/max, distance between buildings, device counts. Structuring
ten of those fields costs nothing and makes site-to-site comparison possible. Right now, comparing
Lunsar's infrastructure to Douala's requires reading two documents and forming an impression.

The overlap with D-1 §4 (per-room power and network) is defensible — per-room versus per-site are
genuinely different — but the boundary should be stated on both pages, or teams will do one and
assume the other is covered.

### D-3 · Workflow mapping

Strong on T1: *"the pain point THEY name"* and the copy chain are what stop a team from digitising a
process nobody follows.

Section 3 — *"which single process could retire its paper register within this deployment, and what
has to be true first"* — is, like B-2's, a testable prediction that nothing ever checks. D-4's phase-1
gate asks whether a unit has retired its paper register, but the two are not joined by any shared
identifier, so a later reader cannot tell whether the process that retired is the process that was
predicted to. Name the process in both places and the join is free.

### D-4 · Phase exit gates

**The most important instrument for T3 and the most under-specified.** Two problems:

**The eight outcomes are named but not operationalised.** Proctor et al.'s eight are the right
framework, and the form gives each a "how measured here" hint — but no definitions, no denominators,
no methods. "Adoption: eligible staff using it weekly" leaves three things open: eligible counted how,
using measured how (system logs? observation? asked?), and weekly over what window. Two cohorts both
writing "60%" would not be reporting the same quantity, and a 2031 comparison across five cohorts
would be comparing five different measurements sharing a label. **This is the specific defect that
most directly defeats the question you asked me about — which steps were key to success.**

**Eight is also too many for the trip you actually have.** The handbook's own §5.3 rule warns that a
half-filled form is worse than none because it looks like data. Eight outcomes measured in the last
week of a three-week deployment will produce two measured, three estimated and three blank. I would
cut the instrument to the four that a departing volunteer can honestly measure — **Adoption, Fidelity,
Penetration, Sustainability** — define those four precisely, and move the other four (Acceptability,
Appropriateness, Feasibility, Implementation cost) to the thesis, where they are analytic categories
rather than field measurements. Fewer numbers that mean something beats eight that do not.

**And outcome 8 cannot be completed by anyone.** "Sustainability: still running at 6/12 months (fill
in later)" — later by whom, onto what, filed where? See §2.

### L-1 · Handover pack

The instrument the evidence most directly supports, and it earns its place. *"What we deliberately did
not do, and why"* is the best single field in the handbook: it is the only place a team is asked to
record a **negative** result, and negatives are what stop the next cohort spending a week rediscovering
a dead end.

Its limitation: **L-1 is a cover sheet for a pack whose contents have no templates.** The readiness
assessment gets a seven-dimension rubric; the runbook — the artefact that actually decides whether a
clerk can operate the system on a Tuesday in March — gets a tickbox. If one thing in the pack deserves
a template, it is the "what to do when it breaks" page, because the handbook already knows its
contents (five failures, first thing to try, the 3–12 minute start-up) and because it is, by the
handbook's own account, the page that gets used more than everything else combined.

### A-1 · Debrief and feedback

Presented as the keystone, and relative to that job it is the weakest form in the set — because
**it asks about the handbook and not about the deployment**.

What it captures: version followed, which instruments were completed or skipped, where the handbook
was wrong, what had to be invented, departure outcome scores, three questions you wish you had asked.
That is a good *product-feedback* form. It is not a *project record*, and the loop needs both.

Missing, in descending order of value:

1. **What did you inherit?** Was there a previous handover pack? Did you find previous instruments?
   Were they usable? What was missing that cost you time? — four lines that turn the thesis's central
   correlation into something measured prospectively from 2027 onward instead of reconstructed from
   2016–2026.
2. **What did you decide that departed from the handbook, and why?** The decision log. Distinct from
   "where the handbook was wrong": a departure may be entirely correct and still needs recording,
   because the next team's first question is *why is it set up this way*.
3. **Who can answer questions about this in a year, and how?** A name and a route with a real
   lifespan, not a graduating student's university address.
4. **What should the next team do first?** L-1 item 9 and D-4's "what was not done" both circle this;
   nothing aggregates it into one sentence a successor will actually read.

## 2. The hole in the middle: nothing exists after week two

This deserves its own section because it is the finding that most directly answers your third
question, and because it is invisible when you read the instruments one at a time.

Three instruments defer to a six- and twelve-month check:

- **D-4**, outcome 8: *"Still running at 6 / 12 months (fill in later)"*
- **A-1**, §5: *"Sustainability (fill at 6 / 12 months)"*
- **L-1**, §4: a table naming who checks at 6 and 12 months, with a date

L-1 is the only one that assigns it, and it assigns it to a person, not to a document. So at month six
there is a name and a date, and **nothing arrives, nothing is filled in, and there is nowhere to put
the answer**. The team has dispersed. In the archive this exact thing already happened: a February
2026 remote check found the server inactive, and it survives as one line in a Word file with no
follow-up. The mechanism the handbook proposes would not have caught that either.

This matters more than it looks, because **survival at 6 and 12 months is the only dependent variable
the whole thesis has**. Handover-correlates-with-survival is a claim about what a project looks like a
year later. Every instrument in the set measures the independent variables — what was done — and none
measures the outcome. A dataset of interventions with no outcomes cannot answer "which steps were
key", however many cohorts it accumulates.

**The fix is a tenth instrument, and it should be deliberately trivial.**

> **S-1 · Six- and twelve-month check.** One page. Ten minutes. Done remotely, by the person named in
> L-1 §4, on the date written there. Designed to be answerable from a WhatsApp exchange with the
> super-user, because that is what will actually happen.
>
> *Is the system running? Who is using it, in which units? Which paper processes are still retired?
> Is the named owner still in post? What has broken since we left, and what happened? B-1 re-scored,
> seven numbers. Anything the hospital wants that it is not getting.*

S-1 is the only instrument whose owner is **not** the departing team. That is precisely why it must be
a separate named artefact with its own code and its own date, rather than a row inside three other
forms — a row inside someone else's form is nobody's job, and this project has extensive evidence
about what happens to things that are nobody's job.

Note the honest limit: S-1 does not need a resolved E2 to work. Its owner is named per-deployment in
L-1, not centrally. That makes it the one piece of the mechanism you can build **while E2 stays
open**, which given today's decision to leave E2 unsettled is worth knowing.

## 3. What is missing that the mission needs

Beyond the instruments.

**3.1 · A level 1–2 path.** B-2 offers four levels and six tools, and argues — correctly, and as its
central contribution — that the right answer is often to deploy *less*. The technical guide then
documents Bahmni and GNU Health: level 4, both of them. A team that completes B-2 honestly and lands
on level 1 or 2 gets a stop rule, a congratulation, and no recipe. **The most likely correct outcome
of the handbook's first substantive instrument is the least supported outcome in the handbook.**

AUCOOP has direct, hard-won experience here — the 2022–23 KoboToolbox deployment, and the January 2024
review that found 2 of 11 forms carrying data, all of it inside two months. That experience currently
appears only as a cautionary statistic. It is also the raw material for the best level-1 recipe anyone
in this space could write: *how to deploy a form-collection system that is still collecting forms a
year later.* Nobody has written that honestly. You can.

**3.2 · Two of the eight organisational recipes produce nothing that survives the trip.**

- **Funding the owner** — the single variable the sustainability argument turns on — is recorded as
  one Y/N in B-1 (`OWNER-5Y`) and one tickbox in B-3. Not the salary figure, not the board's answer,
  not the date to ask again. The recipe tells you to put a costed case to the board and get an answer
  in writing; nothing captures that answer.
- **Training** — whose insufficiency is one of the handbook's own findings — leaves no record of who
  was trained, in what role, by whom, or whether the super-user could run the second session unaided.
  Which means the claim *"training is necessary and demonstrably insufficient"* can never be tested
  again on new data. It will rest on the 2024 certificate forever.

Both are absorbed by the structured record in §5.4 — neither needs a new form.

**3.3 · An arrival brief.** The handbook tells a team how to work and how to leave. It does not tell
them how to *start from what exists* — read the previous cohort's D-1/D-2/D-3, find the handover pack,
check the repositories, ask what was tried before. §6.1 of the brief describes this step; no page in
the handbook does. Given that "each cohort begins from zero" is the entire diagnosis, the absence of a
"before you start" page is conspicuous. It is half a page.

## 4. What is ballast

You asked what is irrelevant. Honestly: very little. Three candidates, in order of confidence.

1. **`aucoop_field_handbook.html` in the repo root.** The superseded single-file first version, 45 KB.
   Two artefacts claiming to be the handbook is exactly the drift problem the handbook is about. Both
   briefs have flagged it; it is still there. Delete it or move it to an `archive/` folder with a
   README saying what it was.
2. **Four of D-4's eight outcomes** — see §1. Not wrong, but they will not be honestly measured in the
   field, and a form that collects three real numbers and five guesses produces something that looks
   like data. They belong in the thesis.
3. **The Story at twelve chapters.** This is *not* ballast — it is the part that transmits judgement
   rather than procedure, and it is the reason the handbook is more than a checklist. But it costs an
   hour, and the reader who most needs it is a volunteer packing at 1 a.m. It needs a **one-page
   version**: twelve lines, one per chapter, printable, in the packing list. Not a replacement — a
   door.

Two things I looked at and concluded should stay as they are: the D-1/D-2 overlap on power and network
(genuinely different scopes, and A-1 would catch a team skipping one), and the glossary and resources
pages (small, and the download table is the page a field team will actually use).

## 5. What to change, in order

Ordered by *analytical value per hour of work*, which is not the order they appear above.

### 5.1 · S-1, the six- and twelve-month check *(new instrument, half a day)*

The one change without which the other three cannot pay off. §2 has the specification. It creates the
outcome variable. It works without E2 being settled.

### 5.2 · Four fields that close four open loops *(an hour)*

| Where | Field |
|---|---|
| **B-2 §3** | Stop-rule conditions ticked · deployed anyway Y/N · by whom · on what grounds · what compensated |
| **A-1 §0** | What we inherited: handover pack found? previous instruments found? usable? what was missing? |
| **A-1** | Departures from the handbook and why (decision log, distinct from "where it was wrong") |
| **B-3 §1** | Did the director announce it to staff — to whom, when, how? |

Each of these records something the archive shows is decisive and that no current field captures.

### 5.3 · Make B-1 an outcome measure, not a screening score *(a day, plus a session with Lunsar and Douala)*

Three columns instead of one — **before travel · at departure · at 12 months** — and anchored
descriptions for 0, 5 and 10 on each of the seven dimensions. Derive the anchors by scoring Lunsar and
Douala retrospectively, which is the same exercise E1 needs. You get the thresholds and the anchors
from one afternoon, and B-1 stops being a conversation aid and becomes the quantitative spine of the
whole evaluation.

### 5.4 · One structured summary sheet per deployment *(a day)*

The change that makes your third question answerable at all.

A single fixed-schema sheet — thirty fields, filed as CSV or JSON beside the DOCXs — carrying the
comparable facts that currently live scattered in nine prose documents:

> site · country · year · cohort · handbook version · B-1 total and seven sub-scores at each of the
> three points · the four survival answers · level chosen · level overridden Y/N · platform · phases
> attempted / closed · paper processes retired, count and names · staff trained by role · super-users
> named Y/N · time allowance agreed Y/N · owner post exists / funded / salary figure / board answer ·
> handover pack left Y/N · previous pack found Y/N · the four D-4 outcomes at departure, 6m and 12m ·
> still running at 12m Y/N

Everything else stays prose, as it should — narrative is where the reasons live. But **thirty
comparable fields across five cohorts is a table you can read in an afternoon**, and it is the
difference between a 2031 analyst reconstructing the pattern archaeologically, as you had to, and
reading it off. It is also generated almost entirely from fields that already exist on the forms; the
work is defining the schema, not collecting more.

Be honest in the handbook about what this can and cannot support: at roughly one deployment a year,
no statistical claim will ever be available. What it supports is **case-comparable analysis** — five
deployments lined up against the same thirty fields, so that what co-occurs with survival becomes
visible. That is exactly the method that produced the handover finding retrospectively. The design
target is to make the next decade produce, prospectively and cheaply, the record you had to
reconstruct by hand.

### 5.5 · Then the rest

- Operational definitions for the four retained D-4 outcomes (denominator, method, window).
- Provenance column throughout D-1; establishment/census/counted distinction forced on capacity.
- A level 1–2 recipe, written from the 2022–23 KoBo experience.
- A "before you start" arrival page.
- Structure ten of D-2's numeric fields.
- A template for the "what to do when it breaks" page.
- A one-page Story card for the packing list.
- Delete `aucoop_field_handbook.html`.

## 6. Answering your three questions directly

**"Would filling these in make the hospital improve faster?"**
Mostly yes, and B-2 is the strongest case: the instrument that prevents a level-4 deployment at a
level-1 site saves a hospital a year of a system nobody uses. B-3 and D-3 aim the work at what staff
say takes too long, rather than what the team arrived wanting to build. The honest caveat is that D-1,
D-2 and D-4 help the *project* rather than the hospital — which is fine and correct, but the handbook
should not imply otherwise, and the "under two hours for all nine" claim in the brief is not credible
against a site with 27 units and fourteen statutory returns. State a realistic per-instrument budget;
a stated budget that is wrong by an order of magnitude is how a team decides the whole set is unserious.

**"Would the next volunteer team have the information they needed?"**
Yes — this is the handbook's strongest dimension, and D-1 plus L-1 would have prevented most of what
the archive shows being lost. One gap: nothing captures the *digital* estate as found, which is the
first thing an arriving team needs and the thing the 2026 cohort most conspicuously lacked.

**"Could you later identify which steps were missing, and which were key to success or obsolescence?"**
**As it stands, no** — and this is the finding worth acting on. Not because the instruments ask the
wrong questions, but because they record only interventions and never outcomes, only single points and
never trajectories, and only prose and never comparable fields. §5.1 through §5.4 fix all three, and
they are four changes, not forty. After them the answer becomes *"yes, at n≈1 per year, as
case-comparable analysis"* — which is modest, honest, and exactly the method the thesis already used
to find the one thing nobody else had found.
